# Carthage: Empire, Constitution, and the Sources

For nearly seven centuries Carthage was the greatest power in the western
Mediterranean. Yet no Carthaginian history survives. Everything we know comes
from Greek and Roman writers — many of them its enemies — who worked from
earlier sources now lost. Read critically, they allow a coherent account of the city's rise, political
order, and destruction; read naively, they repeat the victors' prejudices.

## Origins and the commercial empire

Appian reports two founding traditions: the founders were "either Zorus and
Carchedon, or, as the Romans and the Carthaginians themselves think, Dido, a
Tyrian woman" (*Punic Wars* 1). The ox-hide story — Dido cuts the hide into a strip and encloses the hill that became the Byrsa, "hide" — is an aetiology
of the citadel, not a record of events. Even the date was disputed: Appian puts the
foundation fifty years before the fall of Troy, while the traditional date is
814 BCE.

Carthaginian power is clearer. On the eve of the First Punic War, Polybius says
the Carthaginians "had not only reduced Libya to subjection, but a great part of
Spain besides" (*Histories* 1.10.5). This was an empire of coasts, harbours, and
tribute, not annexed territory.

One text preserves a Carthaginian voice. The *Periplus of Hanno* is a Greek
translation of the record that Hanno, king of the Carthaginians, dedicated in the
temple of Kronos. It records a state-sponsored expedition beyond the Pillars of
Heracles: "It pleased the Carthaginians that Hanno should voyage outside the
Pillars of Hercules, and found cities of the Libyphoenicians" (*Periplus* 1). The
narrative runs down the Atlantic coast of Africa, past a burning land, to an
island of *Gorillai* (15–18).

## The constitution

Carthage impressed Greek thinkers, whose analyses remain our best evidence for
its institutions. Aristotle counts Carthage among the three well-ordered
constitutions, with Sparta and Crete: "The Carthaginians are also considered to
have an excellent form of government" (*Politics* 2.11). He notes kings, a
council of elders, and a popular assembly, and praises electing
magistrates "according to merit." His
strongest proof is stability: the Carthaginians "have never had any rebellion
worth speaking of, and have never been under the rule of a tyrant" (2.11).

Carthage was, Polybius adds, "originally well contrived,"
with kings, an aristocratic council, and a sovereign people (*Histories*
6.51.1–2). But by the Hannibalic War "the Carthaginian constitution had
degenerated, and that of Rome was better" (6.51.3): the multitude had acquired
"the chief voice in deliberations," while at Rome the senate retained it
(6.51.6). His observation that Carthage relied on "foreign and mercenary" troops while Rome used citizens points to a real structural difference
(6.52.4).

## Sicily and the long contest with the Greeks

Carthage's expansion repeatedly brought it into conflict with Greek Sicily.
When the Selinuntians pressed the Egestaeans, the latter "dispatched ambassadors to Carthage, asking for aid and putting their
city in the hands of the Carthaginians," who were "eager to acquire a city so
strategically situated" (*Library of History* 13.43.3–4). After the sack of
Selinus and Himera, he reports, they spared no captives, crucifying
some and inflicting "unbearable outrages" on others (13.111.4). The struggle lasted
two centuries: Dionysius attacked the Carthaginian base at Motya (14.47.4), and
in 310 BCE Agathocles carried the war into Africa itself (20.3.1–3).

## Rome, Hannibal, and destruction

Polybius makes the strategic logic of the First Punic War explicit: the Romans feared that if the Carthaginians took Sicily they would be
"most troublesome and dangerous neighbours, hemming them in on all sides"
(1.10.6). The war lasted twenty-four years and was "the longest, most unintermittent, and
greatest war we know of" (1.63.4–5). Carthage lost Sicily.

Polybius records Hannibal's own account of the oath his father made him swear at
the altar, aged nine, "never to be the friend of the Romans" (3.11.7). Livy
turns Hannibal into a moral portrait: a leader whose "great merits were matched
by great vices — inhuman cruelty, a perfidy worse than Punic" (21.4). "Punic
faith" was a Roman stereotype for treachery, and Livy's characterization owes as
much to Roman moralizing as to his sources.

The end came in 146 BCE. Appian's account of the Third Punic War is our fullest: the siege of Byrsa, the street fighting, the burning of the city, and
the suicide of Hasdrubal's wife (128–131). He records the most famous scene:
Scipio Aemilianus, watching Carthage burn, weeps and quotes Homer on the fall of
Troy, confessing his fear for Rome's own future (132). Rome's relief was immense:
"no other war had so terrified them at their own gates as the Punic wars" (134).

## Conclusion

The sources for Carthage are its enemies'. Aristotle uses the city to test a
theory of constitutions; Polybius to explain Rome's success; Livy to define Roman
virtue; Appian to satisfy a Greek audience fascinated by Rome. Yet the texts agree that Carthage was a maritime
commercial power, governed by magistrates, a senate, and an assembly, reliant on
mercenaries and tribute, and long locked in conflict with Greek Sicily.
Reading the victors against the grain is the only way to recover the city.
