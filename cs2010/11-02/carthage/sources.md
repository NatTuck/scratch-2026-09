Yes, almost all of these ancient texts are in the public domain and freely available online in full-text English translations. 

The direct links to where you can read and cite each source are organized below:

### 1. Polybius, *The Histories*
* **What to look for:** Books 1–3 and scattered later fragments cover the First, Second, and Third Punic Wars. Polybius is generally considered the most analytical ancient author on the conflict.
* **Where to read:** You can access the complete public-domain Loeb translation on the [LacusCurtius edition of Polybius' Histories](https://vertexaisearch.cloud.google.com/grounding-api-redirect/AUZIYQGuUgLku6gDXhaNMXXv-dj2rcTdLZpqHlvs-RQaf6_slQbUnD4AfIZeciYFdXt6-ryEbhEOVQNygYgJpAWG_Muwhe5mhSUStZd0xO_860e-XoiwuYQWTRxt1Dw-FioZXMSYsBroDCSHUPu99dB61FIVTTNNx1UvrcqC3pI=).

### 2. Livy, *Ab Urbe Condita* (History of Rome)
* **What to look for:** Books 21 through 30 provide the most detailed literary narrative of the Second Punic War (Hannibal’s march across the Alps through the Battle of Zama).
* **Where to read:** An English translation by Canon Roberts is hosted at [The Internet Classics Archive at MIT](https://vertexaisearch.cloud.google.com/grounding-api-redirect/AUZIYQFlCG5NqVMyRZA0zoZv1RWHoNWvBTH9v9FgcLpQjNJdMEcv7G1HGVtq9GIHee7cTeIqqYM6vG2WcaWbSz25RyVKSC918DzTmukAerWZ-tgfRaYH4BG9Xrkrhk6Zbg==), and an indexed digital edition is available on [ToposText](https://vertexaisearch.cloud.google.com/grounding-api-redirect/AUZIYQEWvRo4lnUT0jROJFGnAR1hUkwtkcrihI43aTAjNaxohxIviSc9wDpui5l-OSIbwgVUwJ50_a_ObqhdKg6SnCIhjJdHHpZSxrTb9Y-eczyebZfXzE0=).

### 3. Appian of Alexandria, *The Punic Wars*
* **What to look for:** Appian gives the most vivid play-by-play narrative of the Third Punic War, the three-year siege of Carthage, and its fall in 146 BCE.
* **Where to read:** The complete English text is transcribed and divided into chapters on [Livius.org's Appian collection](https://vertexaisearch.cloud.google.com/grounding-api-redirect/AUZIYQFrmJSjCRFyOV77AubnOgmB4ipV9QoSP84b-JcAuP2RqJylScP00iv97GuJEUdyq5BIccA7jLH5FflVuYWs-XxxBFOeTvpuNQs8u4D4QUHHWgAOJ8lYFN8sEndxwhR7_8KWwIJaurv73ePbQLkY7MglMoHSTjJN22q2).

### 5. Aristotle, *Politics* (Book 2, Chapter 11)
* **What to look for:** Book 2, Chapter 11 contains Aristotle’s direct assessment of the Carthaginian constitution, where he analyzes their *Suffetes* (magistrates), Senate, and oligarchical tendencies.
* **Where to read:** Benjamin Jowett's translation of Book 2 is available via [The Internet Classics Archive's Aristotle section](https://vertexaisearch.cloud.google.com/grounding-api-redirect/AUZIYQGAjH6DEjsCIOl1Kw7u8eoky6WU0BjsFz2_RxFfryeXDjzR3uzDwFN8sQyU2qcViJNE21TFCs4goQszXfG4n-M01hx0LfFzkg1ebMsy-GVMecynJQHCColT5U3sgSUIWNvIKeIyjfDZEKm5nQ==).

* **What to look for:** Crucial for writing about Carthaginian expansion in the 5th and 4th centuries BCE, particularly the Sicilian Wars against Syracuse.

### Tips for Citing These in Your Paper
* **Standard Classical Citation:** When quoting ancient authors, it is best practice to cite by **Book, Chapter, and Section** rather than webpage or modern page numbers (e.g., *Polybius, The Histories 1.10.3* or *Aristotle, Politics 2.11*). This allows your instructor to check the passage regardless of which translation edition they use.
* **Translations:** In your bibliography/works cited list, specify the translator (e.g., *Polybius. The Histories. Translated by W. R. Paton. Loeb Classical Library, 1922*).
